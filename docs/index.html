Automated kinematic scoring of tool-use pantomime gestures in limb apraxia

Daanish M. Mulla1, Laurel J. Buxbaum2,3, Aaron L. Wong2,3, & Jonathan A. Michaels1,4,5

Clinical assessment of limb apraxia after stroke relies on slow, offline video coding of tool-use pantomime gestures. Across 53 participants and 39 everyday tools (1,950 trials), we show that 3D upper-limb kinematics and a simple two-pathway ridge model accurately reproduce expert praxis scores (r = 0.87, AUC = 0.95) and generalize to unseen patients.

01 · Task & Movement

What tool-use pantomime looks like in 3D

Participants viewed an image of a tool and demonstrated its use without holding the object while 8 electromagnetic sensors tracked the left hand, arm, and shoulders. Trained coders scored each trial from 0 to 3 across hand posture, arm posture and trajectory, and amplitude and timing—with apraxia deficits (score < 2.52) driven mainly by hand posture errors.

02 · Final Combined Model

Blending global movement profiles with tool-specific models

From each trial we extract 46 scale-free features spanning hand posture, arm coordination, and timing. Combining a participant-level model (overall repertoire mean and variability) with 39 item-level models outperforms either pathway alone—driven most strongly by distal hand kinematics (46.1% of weight).

04 · Tool Battery & Clinical Translation

Which tools separate apraxia—and which predict most reliably

Individual tools vary widely in how well their manual scores alone separate stroke patients with versus without apraxia (manual-score AUC = 0.50–0.82). However, a tool’s learned reliability weight in the model is unrelated to its standalone manual-score apraxia AUC (weight vs. manual AUC: r = 0.10 in training [n = 41], r = 0.01 across all N = 53). Because the item pathway weights tools by within-item score predictability (r = 0.41) rather than group separation, pruning poorly discriminating items (e.g., beer mug, paper clip) while keeping discriminating, predictable tools (e.g., lipstick, iron, nail clipper, corkscrew) offers a practical path toward shorter clinical screening.
